If something seems unsafe

Get people safe first. The system can wait.

  1. Smell of gas

    Leave the home now. Don’t switch lights or appliances on or off, don’t use flames, and don’t use a phone inside. From outside and away from the house, call the gas utility’s emergency line or 911. Only then contact an HVAC company.

  2. Carbon monoxide alarm, or people feeling unwell while heating is running

    Headache, dizziness or nausea count. Get everyone, including pets, outside into fresh air. Call 911. Don’t go back in until emergency responders say it’s safe. Then contact an HVAC company.

  3. Smoke, burning smell, fire, or sparking from equipment

    Leave the home and call 911. Then contact an HVAC company.

  4. Anyone becoming unwell from heat or cold

    Get them to a cooler or warmer safe place and contact medical help or 911 as needed. Then call about the system.

Safe to check

  • The thermostat is set to the right mode (heat or cool) and a sensible temperature.
  • The thermostat has power, or fresh batteries if it uses them.
  • Vents and return grilles in the home aren’t closed or blocked.
  • If you normally change your own filter, whether it looks clogged.
  • Whether a breaker has tripped. Note it and tell the technician. Don’t keep resetting it.

Please leave to a professional

  • Anything involving gas lines, gas valves, burners, pilot lights or combustion equipment.
  • Opening equipment panels, or touching wiring or electrical parts inside.
  • Anything to do with refrigerant or refrigerant lines, including “topping up”.
  • Chipping or melting ice off coils or lines.
  • Restarting or resetting equipment again and again after it shuts itself off.

In very hot or cold weather

  • Never use an oven, stove, grill, camp heater or generator indoors for heat.
  • Keep portable heaters clear of anything that can burn, and follow the maker’s instructions.
  • When it’s hot: use the coolest room, keep blinds closed, drink water, and go somewhere cool if the house can’t be made safe.

Is a heat pump a sensible option for this house?

A heat pump heats and cools with one system, moving heat into the house in winter and out of it in summer. Whether it suits your home depends on the house itself: its size, insulation, existing ductwork and what heats it now. Some homes use a heat pump on its own. Others pair it with a furnace for the coldest days.

We’ll explain which options fit your house and why, and we’ll include the repair option if the current system is worth fixing.

Questions

The AC is running, but the house isn’t cooling. What should I do?

Check that the thermostat is set to cool and a sensible temperature, that it has power, and that vents aren’t blocked. If that doesn’t help, call us and describe what you’re noticing. If you see ice or water on the equipment, don’t chip or clear it. Just tell the technician.

The heat won’t come on. Can I reset the furnace?

Please don’t restart or reset equipment again and again after it shuts itself off, and never work on gas parts or open panels. Check the thermostat’s mode, setting and power, then call us. If you smell gas, leave the home first and call your gas utility or 911 from outside.

Do heat pumps work when it’s really cold?

Many current heat pumps are designed to keep heating in cold weather. How well one suits your home depends on the model, the house and the local climate. Some homes pair a heat pump with a furnace for the coldest days. We’ll explain what makes sense for your house rather than promise numbers.

How often should the system be maintained?

Many homeowners schedule a check before the cooling season and another before the heating season. Your equipment maker’s guidance comes first. Changing a standard filter regularly is something many people do themselves.

What should I do before the technician arrives?

Clear a path to the indoor and outdoor equipment, note what you noticed and when, and have your comfort brief handy if you made one. Please don’t open panels or try repairs.
